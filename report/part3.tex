\section{DeepRitz Method Optimization for the Monodomain Equation}

\subsection{Introduction to the DeepRitz Approach}

The DeepRitz method represents a significant advancement in the numerical solution of partial differential equations (PDEs). Unlike standard neural networks used in PINNs (Physics-Informed Neural Networks), the DeepRitz method is based on the variational formulation of the problem, directly minimizing the energy functional associated with the differential equation. This approach offers several theoretical advantages, especially for problems with Neumann boundary conditions, as in our case of the monodomain equation for cardiac impulse propagation.

\subsection{Initial Architecture and Challenges}

Our initial implementation faced several challenges:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Inadequate wave propagation:} The solution tended to diffuse uniformly without capturing the true dynamics of wave propagation
    \item \textbf{Overfitting to training points:} The model performed well only in the vicinity of collocation points
    \item \textbf{Difficulties with initial conditions:} The network struggled to accurately reproduce the step initial condition
\end{enumerate}

These observations guided a systematic optimization process described in the following sections.

\subsection{Neural Network Architecture Optimization}

The first area of intervention was the neural network architecture. After various experiments, we observed that a deeper network with greater representational capacity was essential to capture the complex spatiotemporal dynamics of the problem.

\subsubsection{Increasing Depth and Capacity}

We progressively expanded the architecture until reaching the optimal configuration:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
layers = [3, 128, 128, 128, 128, 128, 1]
\end{lstlisting}

This new architecture, composed of 5 hidden layers with 128 neurons each, allowed us to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Capture complex temporal dependencies
    \item Model spatial gradients with greater precision
    \item Effectively represent the interaction between diffusion and reaction terms
\end{itemize}

We maintained \texttt{tanh} as the activation function after experiments with \texttt{swish}, \texttt{ReLU}, and custom activations, as it ensured the necessary regularity in the solution and facilitated the calculation of higher-order derivatives.

\subsection{Loss Weight Balancing}

A crucial aspect in optimizing the DeepRitz model was rebalancing the weights assigned to the different components of the loss function. The original formulation assigned equal weights to the terms related to the differential equation (PDE), initial conditions (IC), and boundary conditions (BC).

\subsubsection{Analysis of Individual Components}

Analysis of the individual loss components during training revealed an imbalance: the PDE term decreased much more slowly than the IC and BC terms. This meant that the model was effectively learning to satisfy the initial and boundary conditions, but struggling to respect the differential equation in the domain.

\subsubsection{Progressive Reconfiguration}

Through systematic experiments, we progressively redefined the weights:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
# Loss weights rebalanced to give more importance to the PDE
self.pde_weight = 100.0  # Increased drastically
self.ic_weight = 20.0    # Reduced for balance
self.bc_weight = 20.0    # Reduced for balance
\end{lstlisting}

The new values were determined by analyzing the relative contribution of each term to the total loss and trying to balance the gradient that each component contributed during backpropagation.

This rebalancing significantly improved overall convergence and solution quality, as it forced the network to pay greater attention to the physical dynamics described by the differential equation.

\subsection{Advanced Sampling Strategies}

Another fundamental aspect of our optimization process was improving the sampling strategies for collocation points.

\subsubsection{Increasing the Number of Points}

We progressively increased the number of collocation points:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
n_domain = 8000    # Points in the interior domain
n_boundary = 1600  # Points on the boundary
n_initial = 1600   # Points for the initial condition
\end{lstlisting}

This increase allowed for better coverage of the spatiotemporal domain, reducing the risk of localized overfitting.

\subsubsection{Point Regeneration at Each Epoch}

The real breakthrough was the implementation of dynamic regeneration of collocation points at each training epoch:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def train(..., regenerate_points_per_epoch=True):
    # ...
    for epoch in range(start_epoch, start_epoch + epochs):
        if regenerate_points_per_epoch:
            # Regenerate data at each epoch
            data = self.generate_training_data(n_domain, n_boundary, n_initial, T)
        # ...
\end{lstlisting}

This technique:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Drastically improved the model's generalization to the entire domain
    \item Prevented overfitting on fixed points
    \item Provided more complete coverage of the solution space
    \item Increased model robustness with respect to particular point choices
\end{itemize}

Although this strategy slightly slowed down the training process, the benefit in terms of solution quality was substantial.

\subsection{Optimizer Optimization}

The choice of parameters for the Adam optimizer and scheduler proved crucial for managing the complex parameter space of the neural network.

\subsubsection{Learning Rate and Scheduler}

After numerous experiments, we implemented a more conservative learning rate strategy:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
optimizer = optim.Adam(self.model.parameters(), lr=5e-5)
scheduler = optim.lr_scheduler.StepLR(optimizer, step_size=1000, gamma=0.8)
\end{lstlisting}

The combination of a lower initial learning rate ($5 \times 10^{-5}$ instead of $10^{-3}$) and a scheduler that further reduces the learning rate by 20\% every 1000 epochs ensured:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Greater stability in training
    \item Avoided oscillations in the loss function
    \item Allowed finer convergence in the advanced stages of training
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Implementation of Complete Checkpointing}

A fundamental technical improvement was the implementation of a complete checkpoint system that saves and restores not only the network weights, but also the optimizer state:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
# Save the complete checkpoint
checkpoint = {
    'model_state_dict': self.model.state_dict(),
    'loss_history': self.loss_history,
    'optimizer_state': getattr(self, 'optimizer_state', None),
    'scheduler_state': getattr(self, 'scheduler_state', None),
    'model_params': {
        'sigma_h': self.model.sigma_h,
        'a': self.model.a,
        'fr': self.model.fr,
        'ft': self.model.ft,
        'fd': self.model.fd,
        'pde_weight': self.model.pde_weight,
        'ic_weight': self.model.ic_weight,
        'bc_weight': self.model.bc_weight
    }
}
torch.save(checkpoint, checkpoint_path)
\end{lstlisting}

This technique allowed us to:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Resume training exactly from where it was interrupted
    \item Preserve the moments accumulated by the Adam optimizer
    \item Avoid the ``cold restart'' phenomenon that caused loss spikes when resuming training
    \item Maintain continuity in the learning curve across multiple sessions
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Input Normalization}

Input normalization was another key component of our optimization:
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python]
def normalize_input(self, x, y, t):
    if self.input_normalization:
        x = (x - self.x_mean) / self.x_std
        y = (y - self.y_mean) / self.y_std
        t = (t - self.t_mean) / self.t_std
    return x, y, t
\end{lstlisting}

This normalization ensured that all input variables ($x$, $y$ and $t$) were mapped to the range [-1, 1], significantly improving the convergence of the neural network.

\subsection{Progressive Results Obtained}

The fine-tuning process led to substantial and measurable improvements in solution quality. Here we present the evolution of performance through the main optimization phases.

\subsubsection*{Loss Progression}

The total loss function trend was our main indicator of improvement:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial model}: Final loss $\approx 5 \times 10^{-2}$
    \item \textbf{After architecture optimization}: Loss $\approx 2 \times 10^{-2}$
    \item \textbf{After weight balancing}: Loss $\approx 1 \times 10^{-2}$
    \item \textbf{After Gaussian initial condition}: Loss $\approx 5 \times 10^{-3}$
    \item \textbf{After point regeneration}: Loss $\approx 5.3 \times 10^{-3}$ (on constantly varying points!)
    \item \textbf{After complete optimization}: Loss $< 5 \times 10^{-3}$
\end{itemize}

It's important to note that the final loss obtained with point regeneration represents a qualitatively superior result compared to a slightly lower loss calculated on the same points, as it indicates better generalization.

\subsubsection*{Wave Propagation Quality}

The most significant improvement was in the quality of cardiac wave propagation:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Initial model}: Almost non-existent propagation, uniform diffusion
    \item \textbf{Intermediate phase}: Partial propagation, but with artifacts
    \item \textbf{Optimized model}: Clear wave propagation with influence of regions of different diffusivity
\end{itemize}

The final solution clearly shows the influence of regions with altered diffusivity, corresponding to diseased cardiac tissues, on impulse propagation.
